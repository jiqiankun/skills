## 1. Mode Refinement

### Default Mode

Use the photograph as the obvious hero. Favor a substantial contiguous anchor, clear subject separation, familiar balance when attractive, and a broad but low-density supporting field.

Typical behavior:

- preserve the original crop when it already works;
- use one quiet extension or support field;
- keep paper seams subtle;
- use source-native color unless one added hue clearly improves balance;
- let quiet paper create a finished poster rather than an experimental void;
- omit typography and temporal effects unless genuinely useful.

### Editorial Mode

Use source-driven asymmetry and more deliberate interval design without weakening subject recognition.

Suitable moves:

- offset the photographic anchor toward a source-supported edge or axis;
- create one large calm paper interval and one smaller active interval;
- extend a gaze, path, horizon, silhouette, shadow, light transition, or rhythm through the paper field;
- let one source-derived illustrated mass or underprint connect separated regions;
- use one controlled crop disturbance, interrupted alignment, or unequal margin;
- retain a clear resting point after the subject.

Avoid treating “editorial” as a fixed centered photograph, a detached bright rectangle, a random torn edge, or tiny text beneath an image.

### Strong Collage Mode

Increase transformation while preserving photographic evidence.

- Keep the largest fragment responsible for identity and semantic minimum.
- Connect fragments through one shared source-derived field, rhythm, or chromatic structure.
- Use no more than three photographic fragments unless the user explicitly requests dense montage.
- Keep one fragment clearly dominant; avoid equal scrapbook tiles.
- Protect defining faces, expressions, gestures, architecture, products, food, flowers, and vehicles from destructive overlays.
- Use paper interruption to clarify relationships rather than to scatter visual noise.

## 2. Photo–Illustration Relationships

Choose one primary relationship.

### Support Field

Use when photography is already attractive and needs context, balance, or finish.

- Place one broad low-density field behind, beside, or partly around the photo.
- Derive its boundary from a source silhouette, shadow, light field, horizon, path, or quiet region.
- Keep much of the field unprinted.
- Avoid a generic halo, rectangle, circle, or centered backing card.

### Continuation

Use when a source direction can cross the handoff.

- Extend one contour, axis, interval, rhythm, gap, light transition, or movement.
- Simplify after crossing into paper; do not trace the complete object.
- Let continuity guide the eye while the material boundary remains perceptible.
- Stop before the continuation becomes a decorative border.

### Counterform

Use when a silhouette, opening, shadow, or quiet area can become a strong positive or negative shape.

- Preserve the source relation rather than literal object detail.
- Keep the counterform simple enough to read at thumbnail scale.
- Use it to clarify figure–ground, balance, or breathing room.

### Displacement

Use when repetition or relocation expresses movement, interval, near/far, or before/after.

- Repeat one source-derived form or gap.
- Change scale, spacing, opacity, or position with directional logic.
- Keep the original photographic fact stable.
- Avoid arbitrary echoes and confetti.

### Dissolution

Use only on a secondary fact supported by fading, weathering, motion, memory, haze, reflection, or disappearance.

- Let one contour, texture, or region weaken into paper.
- Preserve the main subject and semantic minimum.
- Use one dissolution behavior; do not combine blur, scratches, fading, distress, and ghosting.

## 3. Illustration Grammars

Choose one primary grammar and at most one supporting grammar.

### Silhouette-Led

Use for figures, trees, roofs, boats, vehicles, products, or strong profiles.

- Build one broad neutral mass.
- Preserve the defining lean, opening, or directional pressure.
- Break selected edges to keep the form paper-native.
- Avoid complete object rendering.

### Contour-Led

Use for architecture, paths, railings, coastlines, hands, gestures, or linear movement.

- Keep only a few broken lines.
- Preserve direction and relationship rather than surface description.
- Vary continuity and pressure.
- Avoid evenly weighted full outlines.

### Field-Led

Use for sky, water, fog, ground, atmosphere, shadow, or broad light behavior.

- Build one irregular ink, halftone, underprint, or exposed-paper field.
- Keep the field broad and active density low.
- Shape its edge from source geometry or light.
- Avoid generic gradient backdrops.

### Rhythm-Led

Use for windows, posts, waves, steps, crowds, branches, traffic, or repeated intervals.

- Compress repetition into a short controlled sequence.
- Vary interval, scale, direction, and absence.
- Include one deliberate break when source-supported.
- Avoid uniform decorative patterns.

### Cut-Paper-Led

Use when the source has a strong shape hierarchy.

- Use one or two simplified cutouts.
- Preserve an identifiable contour, gap, or overlap.
- Keep shadows flat or absent.
- Avoid sticker outlines, lifted corners, or thick layered depth.

## 4. Complexity Compression

Make illustrated detail calmer than photographic detail.

### Foliage and Organic Texture

- Merge overlapping foliage into one dominant canopy or mass and at most one subordinate counter-mass.
- Keep one to three directional branch gestures.
- Use no more than two secondary clusters and one sparse texture sample.
- Omit most individual leaves, needles, fine twigs, vines, grass blades, petals, gravel marks, and hair-like detail.
- Preserve the source-specific lean, opening, branch direction, light gap, or wind behavior.
- If the result resembles lace, coral, engraving, or a botanical print at thumbnail size, merge and remove marks.

### Crowds and Urban Detail

- Preserve the primary person, vehicle, signless structure, path, or density rhythm.
- Merge secondary people, windows, railings, paving, and traffic into grouped masses or intervals.
- Do not invent readable signs, logos, metadata, or faces.
- Preserve meaningful crowding when density is the source tension; reduce micro-detail rather than emptying the scene.

### Architecture

- Preserve perspective, key facade geometry, horizon, structural rhythm, openings, and place-specific silhouette.
- Translate secondary windows or ornament into a short rhythm.
- Keep defining architecture photographic when it carries identity.
- Avoid correcting source asymmetry into generic symmetry.

### Portraits and Products

- Keep face, expression, eyes, hands, anatomy, product form, surface quality, food texture, floral identity, and attractive light photographic.
- Place collage beside, behind, below, or around the defining subject.
- Use illustration to frame gaze, silhouette, gesture, light, or negative space.
- Do not place opaque texture over defining cues.

## 5. Editorial Negative Space

Design quiet paper through contour, interval, pressure, and direction.

- Connect quiet paper to a source edge, gaze, path, horizon, opening, light field, shadow, or off-frame continuation.
- Use one dominant quiet interval and at most one subordinate quiet interval.
- Let the main quiet interval create entry, pause, directional room, or release.
- Keep active marks away from genuine breathing room.
- Allow illustration influence across a broad field while leaving most of it unprinted.
- Treat 25–40% canvas quiet paper as a diagnostic range, with mode-specific budgets from `SKILL.md`.
- Reduce quiet paper when the source meaning depends on crowding, abundance, forest density, celebration, or full-bleed pressure.

Failure signals:

- the photograph looks merely shrunk and parked on blank paper;
- the largest empty region has no directional or emotional relation to the source;
- the eye exits before meeting the subject;
- typography or a color mark exists only to fill an empty corner;
- quiet paper makes the image feel unfinished rather than intentional.

## 6. Material Handoffs

Choose one primary handoff.

### Nearly Seamless Handoff

Use for polished source photography, portraits, products, food, flowers, elegant architecture, or visually complete scenes.

- Use a restrained paper-tone interruption or underprint transition.
- Keep edge activity minimal.
- Preserve clean subject separation and photographic color.

### Quiet Fiber Seam

Use as the normal tactile option.

- Create a narrow exposed-paper interruption with subtle local fiber.
- Keep long calm segments and only one mild pressure point.
- Align the seam with a source edge, direction, or light transition.

### Underprint Passage

Use for strong light, graphic geometry, soft atmosphere, or smooth continuity.

- Pass neutral illustration or one structural hue behind and through selected edge segments.
- Preserve photographic readability.
- Keep underprint texture flat and subordinate.

### Restrained Torn Fracture

Use for movement, fragmentation, directional pressure, or explicit editorial expression.

- Build one visible hand-torn contour with long calm passages and one or two stronger source-derived pressure points.
- Keep the fiber band narrow and local.
- Avoid applying equal tearing to every edge.

### Abraded Transition

Use only when weathering, fading, memory, friction, pressure, or disappearance belongs to the source.

- Use local dry pigment loss, broken emulsion, or worn coverage.
- Keep the defining photographic subject undamaged.
- Avoid generic vintage distress.

Across all handoffs, avoid clean clipping paths, crisp rectangular masks, sticker borders, uniform deckled frames, drop shadows, curled corners, thick paper layers, and 3D mockup depth.

## 7. Color and Vintage Paper

Protect source color first.

### Source-Native Color

Use no added hue when the photograph already has a strong chromatic event, subtle natural color, attractive skin or product color, or an internally complete palette.

### Structural Hue

When needed, choose one print hue by this order:

1. source resonance: intensify a meaningful minor source color;
2. analogous harmony: use a neighboring hue;
3. temperature bridge: add a restrained warm/cool relation;
4. complementary counterpoint: use only when it clearly improves separation or movement.

Choose one integration mode:

- source continuation;
- selective replacement of a secondary region;
- underprint passage;
- counterform;
- directional rhythm.

Require the hue to alter balance, eye path, temperature, continuity, figure–ground, or source meaning. Remove it when the composition remains unchanged without it.

### Vintage Paper Tone

- Use subtle warm cream, soft ivory, faint oatmeal, or restrained aged-paper warmth.
- Apply it to paper, fibers, neutral illustration ink, underprint, and handoff zones.
- Keep it light enough that paper still feels clean and broadly appealing.
- Do not apply a global yellow, brown, faded, or desaturated filter to photography.
- Do not alter skin, sky, food, product, flower, vehicle, or architectural color merely to match paper.
- Avoid stains, heavy foxing, burn marks, dirty beige, or decorative aging.

## 8. Temporal Traces

Use none unless the source supports movement, waiting, weathering, growth, fading, repetition, arrival, departure, passing, or disappearance.

Choose one:

- direction-bound offset repetition;
- slight movement-aligned misregistration;
- weakening contour after crossing the handoff;
- stretched rhythm along motion;
- one fading secondary fragment;
- a repeated interval with one deliberate omission;
- one soft directional echo.

Keep the effect subordinate and clean at first glance. Never combine several aging or ghosting devices to simulate nostalgia.

## 9. Typography

Use no text by default.

When text adds a relation the image cannot carry:

- reproduce user wording exactly;
- otherwise derive one brief concrete or sensory fragment from object, weather, movement, light, distance, touch, material, or atmosphere;
- use the language implied by the user and source context;
- keep it small, legible, paper-integrated, and subordinate;
- align it with a source edge, torn edge, baseline, axis, or quiet pocket;
- treat it as the final resting point, never the first entry;
- use one lettering world: restrained handwriting, typewriter-like letterpress, faint pencil, worn stamp, dry ink, or quiet print;
- avoid generic poetry, invented quotations, faux metadata, dates, coordinates, serial numbers, labels, and polished display type.

Remove text when silence is stronger or spelling cannot be verified.

## 10. Targeted Correction Matrix

Correct one dominant failure and regenerate at most once.

- **Weak first-glance appeal:** simplify competing systems, clarify the subject, restore harmonious color, and choose the more satisfying balance.
- **Photography reduced:** restore a larger or stronger coherent photo anchor, natural color, texture, light, perspective, focus, depth, and context.
- **Subject weakened:** restore expression, silhouette, scale, separation, tonal clarity, attractive light, and defining cues; reduce overlays.
- **Scene loss:** restore the missing truth invariant, semantic minimum, or key spatial relationship.
- **Over-artistic result:** select the clearer, more flattering, more harmonious direction and retain only one quiet authorial residue.
- **Generic prettiness:** restore one source-specific asymmetry, interruption, interval, color relation, material event, gap, or unresolved relation without reducing appeal.
- **Over-literal illustration:** remove detail, merge forms, break contours, and enlarge breathing room.
- **Generic abstraction:** replace invented motifs with source-derived contour, light, axis, interval, rhythm, direction, gap, movement, or pressure.
- **Illustration too timid:** enlarge its field or dominant mass without adding descriptive detail.
- **Illustration competes:** lower contrast, active density, saturation, or edge activity; move it away from defining cues.
- **Too empty:** restore a supporting field, tonal relation, rhythm, secondary mass, or source-derived continuation without arbitrary decoration.
- **Crowding:** remove competing marks and micro-detail while preserving meaningful source density.
- **Weak hierarchy:** strengthen the subject or defining relationship and simplify subordinate systems.
- **Contrast competition:** choose one primary event and quiet at least two supporting systems.
- **Harsh color:** shift toward source resonance, analogous harmony, or a restrained temperature bridge.
- **Decorative color:** redesign the hue so it changes balance, continuity, eye path, temperature, or figure–ground; otherwise remove it.
- **Photography recolored:** restore source-native photographic color and confine vintage warmth to paper systems.
- **Unjustified handoff:** replace tearing or abrasion with a nearly seamless handoff, quiet seam, or underprint.
- **Edge noise:** restore long calm segments and confine stronger rupture to one or two source-derived points.
- **Decorative aging:** remove generic stains, scratches, fading, ghosting, distress, and misregistration.
- **Text failure:** restore supplied wording exactly; otherwise simplify, reduce contrast, move to breathing room, or remove it.
- **Overdesigned result:** remove the least necessary intervention and reassess the complete image.

## 11. Final Advanced Checks

- Does the active mode match explicit user intent?
- Do photography, quiet paper, illustration field, and active ink follow perceptual rather than arithmetic budgeting?
- Is at least 25% quiet paper present in ordinary use, or is an allowed dense/full-bleed exception clearly source-supported?
- Does the subject remain more memorable than collage technique?
- Does source light remain credible and attractive?
- Does one source-derived logic connect photography, illustration, color, and paper?
- Is one contrast event dominant?
- Are at least two supporting systems quiet?
- Does vintage warmth remain outside the defining photographic subject?
- Is any visible tear, abrasion, time effect, hue, or text necessary?
- Does the result retain one quiet source-specific residue without sacrificing immediate appeal?
